Reading alone does not build a vocabulary

You finish the article and the unfamiliar words are still unfamiliar. Three reasons why.

You read straight past the words you do not know

Stopping for every unfamiliar word breaks the flow, so you keep going. By the end of the page you cannot name a single word you skipped.

Without the sentence, the definition does not stick

You look the word up somewhere else, read one line, and close the tab. The context that made it memorable is gone.

Every word looks equally worth learning

A word used forty times on a page will meet you again next week. A word used once probably will not. A plain list of words cannot tell you which is which.

Notes you copy by hand never get opened again

Typing definitions into a document takes longer than the reading did, and the file ends up somewhere you never look.

How it works

Three steps, all on the page you are already reading.

  1. 1

    Scan the page

    Click the button in the bottom-left corner. WordLens walks the visible text once, counts every English word in your browser, and shows progress while it works.

  2. 2

    See the words ranked by frequency

    The results open in a new tab, ordered by how often each word appears. Common stop words and very short words are filtered out by default, and the filters are yours to change.

  3. 3

    Look up and export the ones you want

    Click a word for its definition and examples, check the words worth keeping, then export them as a Markdown file you can keep or share.

Features

Everything the extension does, and nothing it does not.

Scan any page

A scan-style button sits in the bottom-left corner of every page. One click reads the visible English text; the icon sweeps while it works and a short toast reports what it found.

Frequency-ranked word list

The scan opens the extension's own results page in a new tab, listing every English word sorted by how often it appears on the page you just read. Common stop words and words shorter than three characters are filtered out by default, with toggles to change both.

Smart merging

Word forms are merged, so running, runs, and ran count as one entry and the ranking reflects the words you met rather than the forms they arrived in. Turn the merging rule off to see every surface form on its own.

Instant definitions

Click any word to see its phonetic transcription, part of speech, definitions, and example sentences, plus audio pronunciation when the source provides it. Definitions come from free, open English dictionary sources, and the panel labels which source each result came from.

Pick and export

Check the words worth keeping, or select all, invert the selection, or take just the current filtered results. Export writes a Markdown file that renders correctly in Typora, VS Code, and on GitHub.

Fast and searchable

The search box filters the list instantly as you type, and sorting switches between frequency and alphabetical. The list stays smooth with several thousand words on screen.

Private by design

Page text is never collected or uploaded, and all counting happens locally in the browser. The only outbound network requests are dictionary lookups for words you actually click.

No account, no key, no cost

Definitions come from free, open dictionary sources. There is nothing to sign up for, no API key to paste, and no paid tier to unlock the parts that matter.

Comfortable UI

Dark and light mode follow your system setting. Arrow keys move through the list, Enter opens a word, and Esc closes it.

Install

Two ways in. The Chrome Web Store listing is not live yet, so start from the source.

From source

Works today

The extension has no build step and no dependencies. Chrome loads the folder as it is.

  1. Download or clone the extension repository

    Grab the ZIP from github.com/Jsn-An/high-frequency-word-scanner and unzip it, or clone it with git clone. Keep the data folder next to manifest.json if you want the optional offline dictionary; the extension loads without it too.

  2. Open chrome://extensions in Chrome

    Type the address into the URL bar and press Enter. It is a browser-internal page, so it cannot be linked from here.

  3. Turn on Developer mode

    The switch is in the top-right corner of that page. Three developer buttons appear once it is on.

  4. Click Load unpacked and select the extension folder

    Choose the folder that contains manifest.json — the repository root, not the docs folder you are reading now.

  5. Pin it to the toolbar if you like

    Pinning is optional. Either way, the scan button appears in the bottom-left corner of pages automatically — there is no toolbar popup to open first.

Requirements

  • Chrome 114 or newer on desktop.
  • No account, no sign-in, and no API key.
  • An internet connection for definitions only. Scanning and counting work offline.

Privacy

The short version: the pages you read stay on your computer.

  • No data collection, no analytics, no accounts. The extension has no sign-in and no telemetry. The counting happens in the tab you are reading, and the result is stored on your own device so the results page can restore it after a refresh.

  • Page text is never uploaded. No page content, page URL, or surrounding sentence is ever sent anywhere. Nothing about the article you read leaves your browser.

  • The only outbound requests are dictionary lookups for words you click. A lookup sends the single word — nothing else — to a free open dictionary, and the answer is cached so the same word is never fetched twice. Open a word offline and the panel tells you the lookup failed instead of hanging.

  • This page collects nothing either. No cookies, no analytics script, no third-party requests, and no fonts or images loaded from anywhere else.

Questions

Does it work on any website?

It runs on any http or https page, including pages behind a login, because the scan happens in your own tab. Chrome does not allow extensions to run on its internal pages, so the button will not appear on chrome:// settings, the Chrome Web Store, or other extensions' pages.

Does it send my browsing data anywhere?

No. The text is counted in your browser and the result stays on your device. The only requests the extension makes are dictionary lookups for words you click, and each of those carries the single word, not the page, the URL, or the sentence it came from.

Why is my word count different from the words I can see?

Four things usually explain the gap. Hidden elements, scripts, and styles are skipped entirely. Code blocks are excluded by default, so identifiers from examples are not counted. Word forms are merged into one entry. And stop words plus words shorter than three characters are filtered out of the list by default, though their counts are still behind the toggles.

On a very long page the scan stops at 200,000 characters so the tab stays responsive, and the results page says so in a banner when that happens.

Can it handle any language?

It looks for English words, so on a bilingual page the ranking reflects the English text and the rest is ignored. Digits, URLs, email addresses, and filenames are skipped as well, which keeps host names and file extensions out of the list.

Does it need an internet connection?

Scanning, ranking, filtering, and searching all work offline. Definitions are fetched from free online dictionaries, so you need a connection to open a word or to export a file with definitions in it. Words whose definitions have not loaded still export with their word and count.

Is it free?

Yes. There is no paid tier, no account, and no API key. The dictionary sources it reads from are free and open, which is also why the extension can work without you signing up for anything.

Do I have to pick words one at a time?

No. You can select all, clear, invert the selection, or select just the results of the filter you are currently looking at. If you export with nothing selected, the extension offers to export everything and tells you how many words that is, rather than guessing.

Where do the definitions come from?

From free, open English dictionary sources: Wiktionary and WordNet. The detail panel labels the source of each result, so you can tell where a definition came from. A word that no source knows is reported plainly instead of leaving an empty panel.
